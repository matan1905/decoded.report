Every time a company officer, director or large shareholder trades the company's stock, the public finds out within two business days. That is fast, and it is why insider filings are watched so closely. The catch is that most of those filings are not trades in any meaningful sense. They are grants, vestings and tax withholdings dressed up as transactions. Reading the codes is what separates signal from paperwork.

The forms and the clock

Insiders file three forms with the SEC. Form 3 is the initial statement of ownership, due within ten days of becoming an officer, director or holder of more than ten percent of a class. Form 4 reports changes in ownership and is due within two business days of the transaction. Form 5 is an annual catch-up for certain exempt transactions, due within forty-five days after the fiscal year ends. Form 4 is the timely one and the one people mean when they say "insider filing."

Transaction codes: the letters that matter

Each line on a Form 4 carries a one-letter code describing the nature of the transaction. The SEC's own investor bulletin lists the common ones. Here is what they actually tell you.

  • A: grant or award. The insider received shares or options from the company as compensation. Not a purchase. Not a bullish signal by itself.
  • P: open-market or private purchase. The insider spent their own money to buy. This is the code people care about, because it is the strongest evidence that an insider sees value at the current price.
  • S: open-market or private sale. The insider sold. It matters, but context decides: planned diversification, taxes and estate planning all show up as S.
  • M: exercise or conversion of a derivative. The insider exercised options or converted a security. Usually compensation mechanics, not a market purchase.
  • F: shares withheld for tax or exercise price. Often misread as a sale. It is the company withholding shares to cover taxes or the cost of an exercise, and it is mechanical. Do not treat F like S.
  • D: disposition to the issuer. Shares sold or transferred back to the company, not to the market.
  • G: gift. A transfer without consideration. Reportable, usually not directional.
  • K: equity swap or hedging transaction. Derivative positions. Worth a look when it appears.
  • J: other. A catch-all with a footnote explaining it. Read the footnote.
  • V: voluntary early report. Something reportable on Form 5 that the insider chose to report early.

Two traps. First, the code is not the same as the acquired/disposed flag sitting in a different column, and neither is the same as the direct/indirect ownership flag. Copy the whole row, not the letter. Second, a code A grant followed by a code F withholding can leave the insider owning more shares than before while the headline screams "disposed." Read the net.

When selling actually matters

  • Cluster selling. Several insiders selling in the same window is more meaningful than one.
  • Large sales relative to the insider's holdings. A director selling most of their position is different from selling a slice.
  • Sales with no pre-arranged plan. Many outsiders sell under a Rule 10b5-1 plan, a pre-set schedule adopted in advance to avoid any appearance of trading on inside information. Sales under a plan are less informative about current sentiment, because the decision was made earlier.
  • Sales into a rally. Dumping into strength reads differently than selling into weakness.

When buying actually matters

Open-market purchases (code P) are rarer than sales. Insiders have many reasons to sell and few to buy more, so a genuine P buy, especially a large one by a senior officer, is a stronger signal than any sale is a warning. Even then, a purchase is a research lead, not a conclusion. It tells you what one person did with their own money on one day.

How to read a Form 4 in a minute

  1. Read the transaction codes on every line. Ignore A, M, F and G lines for the signal.
  2. For a P or S line, note the number of shares, the price, and the ownership after the trade.
  3. Check the footnote for a 10b5-1 plan reference.
  4. Compare the filing date to the transaction date. Late filings are their own small flag.

The report summarizes the insider activity as a net flow over time, which filters out grants and tax withholdings so the chart reflects real market intent.
