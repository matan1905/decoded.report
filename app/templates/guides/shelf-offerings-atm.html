If dilution is the disease, the shelf registration is the prescription the company writes for itself in advance. It is the paperwork that lets a company sell shares later without warning, and learning to read it turns "why did the stock drop on no news" into a question with a document behind it.

Shelf registration, in plain terms

US companies register securities before they sell them. Two forms do most of the work. Form S-1 is the general registration statement, used by companies that do not yet qualify for the faster form. Form S-3 is the short form, available to companies that meet certain eligibility conditions including timely filing of their reports. Filing an S-3 is not a sale. It is the company placing a shelf of registered securities that it can draw down later.

Under SEC Rule 415, a shelf registration can stay effective for up to three years. During that window the company can sell in pieces, timing each sale to its needs and the market. The registered amount is a ceiling, not a commitment. Unused capacity simply expires when the shelf does.

This is why an S-3 filing moves a stock even when no shares are sold. The market reads the shelf as an option to dilute, and prices that possibility in. Traders call the drag from that overhang.

Follow-on offering: the shelf drawn down

When a company actually sells off the shelf, it files a prospectus supplement. Form 424B5 is the one you will see most often for a new sale. It states the number of shares, the offering price or the pricing mechanism, and the underwriter or sales agent. If you only set one alert on a company's EDGAR feed, make it this one: a 424B5 is dilution happening now.

A follow-on offering usually prices at a discount to the last close to attract buyers. That discount plus the new supply is why the stock often drops on the announcement.

At-the-market (ATM) programs

An ATM is a shelf sale stretched over time. The company engages a sales agent, usually a bank, to sell newly issued shares into the ordinary market at whatever the price is on the day of the sale. There is no single block trade and often no single headline. The company must disclose the total amount it may sell under the program, but it is not required to announce each day's sales.

For a holder the effect is the same as a follow-on: more shares, each worth less of the company. The difference is that the dilution is quiet, and the price tends to drift down gradually rather than gap down once. The way to catch it is the quarterly report, which discloses how many shares were sold under the ATM during the period.

The "baby shelf" limit

Smaller companies face a cap on how much they can sell off an S-3 in a given year. Under the general instructions to Form S-3, a company that does not meet a public float threshold may sell only up to one third of its public float in any twelve-month period using that form. This is the baby shelf rule. It limits how fast a small company can drain its shelf, which is why very small issuers often use S-1 registration, private placements, or convertible debt instead. Knowing which of these a company relies on tells you a lot about where the next shares will come from.

How to track them on any ticker

  1. Find the shelf. Search EDGAR for the company's Form S-3 and read the registered amount and the expiration.
  2. Watch for 424B5. Each one is a sale off the shelf or an ATM program launch.
  3. Read the 10-Q for "at the market" language. That is where the cumulative ATM sales show up.
  4. Track the share count. Quarter over quarter, as in How to Spot Share Dilution.
  5. Check the financing exhibits. Convertible notes and equity lines move shares too, and they leave terms in the exhibits that a shelf alone does not.

The one question that separates good from bad

Shelf capacity is a tool. A company that raises money to fund a specific, creditable plan is using the tool well. A company that draws on the shelf every quarter just to keep the lights on is on a treadmill, and the share count is the odometer. The filing tells you how. The use-of-proceeds section tells you why. Read both.
