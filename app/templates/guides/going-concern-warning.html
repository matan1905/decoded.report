A going concern warning sounds like a bankruptcy filing. It is not. It is a specific accounting disclosure, written to a fixed standard, that says management has substantial doubt about the company's ability to pay its bills for the next twelve months. Understanding what the phrase actually commits the company to say, and what it does not, stops you from misreading both the danger and the false alarms.

Where the rule comes from

The standard is ASC 205-40, issued by the FASB in 2014 and effective for annual periods ending after December 15, 2016. It puts the assessment in management's hands and requires it every annual and interim reporting period. Management must look forward one year from the date the financial statements are issued and decide whether conditions and events, taken together, make it probable that the company cannot meet its obligations as they come due.

That word "probable" matters. Substantial doubt is not a coin flip and it is not a general worry about the business. It is a defined threshold. Auditors apply a parallel standard, PCAOB AS 2415, and when the doubt is not resolved the auditor's report carries an explanatory paragraph. So a going concern sentence can come from management's own footnote, from the auditor's opinion, or both.

What triggers it

The classic pattern is a company with recurring losses, negative operating cash flow, and not enough runway to cover the next year. Add a debt maturity it cannot refinance, a covenant it is about to break, or a supplier it cannot pay, and the assessment moves toward substantial doubt.

The disclosure is required in two situations:

  • Doubt raised and alleviated. Management identifies substantial doubt, then explains the plans that resolve it (a signed financing, a completed offering, a credible commitment). The footnote describes the conditions and the plans, but does not have to state that doubt remains.
  • Doubt raised and not alleviated. Management must state plainly that there is substantial doubt about the ability to continue as a going concern within a year, and lay out the conditions, the plans, and the risks.

Why it is not the same as "it will die"

Plenty of companies raise a going concern flag, fix the balance sheet, and keep operating. The disclosure is a snapshot of liquidity against obligations on the day the numbers were issued. A company that closes a financing right after the period end can wipe the doubt away in the next report. The phrase is a warning that the arithmetic of the next twelve months is tight, not a verdict.

What it does tell you is the direction of travel. A first-time going concern paragraph usually signals that the company will need outside money, and outside money for a company in this position usually means new shares. That is the bridge between this guide and dilution: the warning often predicts the offering.

How to read it without overreacting or underreacting

  1. Check who said it. Management's footnote, the auditor's opinion, or both. An auditor paragraph that remains after management's plans is the more serious version.
  2. Read the plans. "We believe we will raise additional capital" is a hope. "We closed a $20 million offering on this date" is a fact. The standard requires the difference to be visible.
  3. Do the runway math. See Cash Runway. Going concern and a short runway are the same fact told two ways.
  4. Watch what happens next. The next 8-K, the next S-3, the next quarter's share count. The disclosure predicts a financing; the filings confirm it.

A note on language

You will see the phrase in filings as "substantial doubt about the Company's ability to continue as a going concern." When it appears without qualification, it is the ASC 205-40 language for doubt that management did not alleviate. When it appears inside a discussion of plans, read carefully: the standard lets a company disclose conditions and remedies even when the doubt itself was resolved. The presence of the words is not the signal. Whether the doubt was alleviated is.
